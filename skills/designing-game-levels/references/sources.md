# Sources

Accessed 12 August 2026. This package independently synthesizes named-practitioner level-design
methods, collaborative professional references, usability research, and iterative studio practice.
It does not adapt AAAbench. Level methods are strongly genre-, camera-, mechanic-, audience-, and
production-dependent; the cited sources support a workflow, not universal room sizes or pacing
formulas.

## Metrics, blockout, and iteration

- The Level Design Book,
  [Blockout](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout) and
  [Metrics](https://book.leveldesignbook.com/process/blockout/metrics),
  is an open collaborative practitioner handbook explaining gameplay metrics, grayboxing, scale,
  collision, sightlines, and cheap iteration before decoration. It supports the spatial-contract
  workflow and the movement-metric good/bad pair. Its examples lean toward 3D practice and are not
  experimental proof of one process.
- Valve,
  [“The Cabal: Valve's Design Process for Creating Half-Life”](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-cabal-valve-s-design-process-for-creating-half-life),
  documents a shipped team's iterative level-design process, shared constraints, playtesting, and
  revision. It supports testing integrated spaces early; it is a retrospective from one studio and
  era, not a staffing or schedule prescription.
- Valve,
  [“Half-Life 2's Design Process”](https://cdn.steamstatic.com/apps/valve/2006/GDC2006_HL2DesignProcess.pdf)
  (GDC 2006), describes measurable experience goals, small increments, external playtests, silent
  observation, and iteration. It supports the validation and telemetry limits in this package. Its
  cadence and resources are project-specific.
- Steve Lee,
  [“An Approach to Holistic Level Design”](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1024774/An-Approach-to-Holistic-Level)
  (GDC 2017), is practitioner guidance on building levels around the total game experience rather
  than isolated encounters. It supports tracing space to player verbs, camera, pacing, and
  progression; it is not a controlled comparison.

## Guidance, learning, and encounters

- Emilia Schatz,
  [“Level Design Workshop: Solving Puzzle Design”](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1025756/Level-Design-Workshop-Solving-Puzzle)
  (GDC 2019), offers named-practitioner methods for puzzle goals, states, communication, iteration,
  and failure. It supports explicit puzzle state and recovery contracts; examples do not transfer
  automatically across genres.
- Worch and Smith,
  [“What Happened Here? Environmental Storytelling”](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1012647/What-Happened-Here-Environmental)
  (GDC 2010), discusses how spatial evidence and player interpretation can convey story. It supports
  deliberate information placement and observation of player inference, not making every space
  narratively dense.
- Pinelle, Wong, and Stach,
  [“Heuristic Evaluation for Games”](https://doi.org/10.1145/1357054.1357282)
  (CHI 2008), derives game-usability heuristics from 108 PC-game reviews and a small preliminary
  evaluation. It supports consistent navigation, status, feedback, and recovery checks; it does not
  establish enjoyment or modern cross-platform behavior.

## Good/bad mapping and limits

- The measured-gap pair applies the Level Design Book's metrics-first blockout method and Valve's
  iterative test practice. The example does not prescribe a particular jump formula.
- The teach/practice/test pair is a conservative synthesis of Valve's incremental observation,
  Schatz's state-based puzzle iteration, and Pinelle et al.'s usability boundary. It deliberately
  rejects a rigid room count because no cited source establishes one.
- The final-fidelity retest rule follows Valve's integrated iteration and the Level Design Book's
  distinction between blockout and later production; specific art/audio effects remain project
  hypotheses.
